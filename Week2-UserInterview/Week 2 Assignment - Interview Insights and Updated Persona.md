# Week 2 Assignment: Interview Insights, Updated Problem Statement, and Persona

## Interview Synthesis

### Participants

- **Henry:** One of the AI content team's main producers. He leads project organization and coordinates production across the team.
- **Eddy:** A short-term freelance VFX specialist who supports difficult AI shots, compositing, and post-production.
- **Jwan:** An AI content creator who produces assigned shots independently or with a small team. He described working with teams of about three to four people.

### Shared Production Context

Projects begin with the client, producer, art director, and team leads refining an art treatment. The AI production team then reviews the scope, divides shots, prepares assets, and tracks assignments in Notion. Creators generate and refine shots using a combination of AI tools and post-production software. Shots are uploaded to Frame for art direction review, then edited and reviewed again by the client and production leads. Slack, Gmail, Notion, Frame, local folders, and model-specific platforms all play a part in the workflow.

### Key Patterns Across Interviews

1. **Shot creation involves many iterations.** Henry described time-consuming still-image generation and batches of prompt/reference combinations. Eddy described splitting complex shots into sections and compositing them. Jwan estimated about 10–20 generation attempts per shot, often in batches.
2. **Generation context is difficult to preserve.** The relationship between a final shot, its prompt, reference images, model settings, and intermediate assets can become unclear, especially during detailed revisions or handoffs.
3. **Handoffs can trigger repeated work.** Creators may receive source materials without an easy way to review earlier generation results or understand exactly which inputs produced them. They may need to test the materials again before continuing.
4. **Project status is spread across tools and conversations.** Notion helps track assignments, while Slack and Gmail carry updates and files, and Frame hosts review material. Team members may still need to ask one another who is working on what or what specifically needs to change.
5. **Feedback timing affects effort and progress.** A creator may spend substantial time generating a video before learning that the visual direction is not approved. In some cases, an early still image may be enough to decide whether to continue.
6. **Current workarounds depend on individual habits.** Participants use local folder structures, personal naming conventions, verbal questions, their own prompt-writing methods, and model-platform histories to fill workflow gaps.

## 1b. Insights About Your Target User

### 1. User Backgrounds

The people involved in professional AI filmmaking have different but connected roles. The core production lead organizes the project and coordinates with producers, art directors, editors, clients, and creators. AI creators produce assigned shots, either independently or with a small team. VFX specialists may join temporarily to handle complex compositing and post-production. The interviews covered a small team of about three to four people and projects ranging from roughly 40–50 shots to batches of 10–20 shots assigned to one specialist.

### 2. Current Experience

The production process usually starts with meetings to refine an art treatment. The team then reviews scope, divides shots, prepares characters or other assets, and tracks work in Notion. Creators use AI image and video tools to generate multiple options, select promising results, revise prompts or references, and upload shots to Frame for review. Approved material moves to editing and later client or producer review.

Work is distributed across several tools: Notion for task tracking, Slack or Gmail for communication and file sharing, Frame for review, local folders for assets, and AI generation platforms for prompts and outputs. When a handoff lacks a clear record of the earlier result and its inputs, the next person may need to reopen files, ask colleagues, or rerun generations to understand how to proceed.

### 3. Motivation

Users want to deliver visually strong work that follows the art treatment and the client's requirements, including accurate product or location details. They want to move through shot production efficiently, preserve successful creative decisions, and make it easier for teammates to continue one another's work. Production leads also need a clear view of assignments and progress so they can coordinate a small team and respond before substantial effort is spent in the wrong direction.

### 4. Barriers to Change

- Generation is unpredictable: an approach that seems promising may still produce unusable results after many attempts.
- Successful prompts, reference images, settings, and intermediate assets are not always linked to the final shot in a consistent way.
- Team members use different personal workflows and naming conventions, while project information is spread across multiple tools.
- Handoffs may include source materials but not a clear, reviewable history of what was generated and what was approved.
- Feedback may arrive only after a still has been turned into a video or after a creator has spent much of a day on a shot.
- Creators may not ask for missing information; they often work around gaps themselves by testing again or rewriting prompts.

### 5. Definition of Success

Interviewees did not state numeric success targets. Based on their experiences, success would mean that a teammate can identify the exact prompt, reference images, settings, and intermediate assets behind a shot; continue a handoff without repeating avoidable tests; find the current owner and status of a shot; and receive feedback early enough to redirect work before producing a full video.

**Candidate measures to validate:** time needed to locate a shot's generation history; number of repeated generation attempts caused by an incomplete handoff; time between a creator's first reviewable still and art direction feedback; and time needed to identify a task owner and current status. These are proposed measures based on interview evidence, not targets stated by participants.

## 2a. Updated Problem Statement

**By helping small AI filmmaking teams start capturing and sharing each shot's prompts, reference images, generation settings, assets, and review status in one shared workflow, we will reduce repeated testing and avoidable rework so teams can deliver client-approved shots more efficiently.**

This statement reflects recurring interview evidence: creators often cannot trace a selected result back to the exact prompt and reference combination; handoffs may omit generation context; missing intermediate assets can force people to recreate work; and late feedback can lead to wasted generation time. The interviews support the problem and desired direction, but they do not establish a numeric improvement target.

## 2b. Updated Persona

### Henry — AI Production Lead

**Role:** One of the main producers on a small professional AI filmmaking team  
**Experience context:** Leads project organization and helps coordinate shot production with creators, art direction, producers, editors, and clients. The Week 1 persona lists Henry as 28, based in New York, with a master's degree and two years of experience; these demographic details were not reconfirmed in the interviews.

### About

Henry leads the organization of AI filmmaking projects but depends on a small team to produce the shots. He helps translate an approved art treatment into a production plan, clarifies responsibilities, and coordinates review. The team may produce dozens of shots per project and use several AI generation and post-production tools.

### Goals

- Turn an approved art treatment into a clear shot plan with owners and timelines.
- Help the team produce shots that meet both client requirements and the studio's visual standards.
- Preserve the prompts, references, settings, and intermediate assets behind successful shots.
- Make handoffs clear enough that collaborators can continue work without reconstructing the process.
- See project ownership and progress across the team.

### Behaviors

- Starts projects with kickoff and art treatment review.
- Uses Notion to organize scenes, shots, camera movement, responsibilities, and status.
- Uses Slack and Gmail to communicate and share files, and Frame to collect visual feedback.
- Uses AI tools and prompt skills to develop prompts and generate multiple options.
- Relies on creators to evaluate their own shots before formal art direction review.

### Frustrations

- Generation histories do not always clearly connect a successful output to its prompt, reference image, settings, or source elements.
- Detailed revisions can cause intermediate assets or useful process information to disappear.
- A handoff may contain many files but still leave the next person unsure which combination produced the selected result.
- Teammates may need to repeat tests or rebuild elements before they can continue.
- Different individual project-management habits make it hard to see who is working on what or who has capacity.

### Needs

- A reliable shot-level record that connects versions, prompts, references, model settings, and intermediate assets.
- A handoff view that makes the selected result and its generation history easy to understand.
- A shared view of shot ownership, status, and team workload.
- Earlier review points so the team can validate a still or visual direction before spending time generating a full video.

### Evidence-Based Update

The Week 1 persona described repeated copy-and-paste work, difficulty tracking prompts for successful shots, difficult handoffs, and challenges managing multiple projects. Interviews with Henry, Eddy, and Jwan support and expand those hypotheses: the problems occur across production, specialist handoffs, asset retrieval, and review timing. The interviews also show that early feedback and visibility into task ownership matter alongside prompt and asset management.
